6. Course Description 
This advanced doctoral-level course develops a theoretically rigorous and research-intensive 
interdisciplinary framework for examining the entanglements between ecological degradation, 
environmental change, illness, and planetary wellbeing across human and more-than-human worlds. It 
approaches “ecosickness” as a critical and interpretive concept through which illness can be understood 
not merely as an individual biomedical condition but as an embodied, ecological, cultural, political, and 
planetary phenomenon. The course brings Environmental Humanities into sustained dialogue with 
Planetary Health, Health Humanities, Medical Humanities, One Health, Political Ecology, 
Environmental History, Posthumanism, New Materialism, Earth-system science, Science and 
Technology Studies, and Critical Global Health. 
A central intervention of the course is its interrogation of the historical separation between human health 
and environmental health that has structured modernity, public health, medicine, development, and 
environmental governance. It critically examines human exceptionalism, biomedical reductionism, and 
the nature–culture divide, asking how the health of human bodies is inseparable from the health of 
ecological systems. The course examines how climate change, biodiversity loss, pollution, toxicity, 
extractivism, altered disease ecologies, food and water insecurity, pandemics, urbanization, and 
environmental disasters become embodied as illness, vulnerability, displacement, anxiety, grief, and 
social suffering. Particular attention is given to the uneven distribution of environmental health risks 
through caste, class, gender, indigeneity, disability, geography, coloniality, and political economy. The 
course therefore asks not only who becomes sick under conditions of environmental change, but also 
whose suffering becomes visible, whose knowledge counts, and how environmental futures are shaped 
through unequal relations of power and expertise.

The course moves from the individual body to the social body, ecological body, and planetary body, 
asking how bodies become sites where environmental histories are registered. Key concepts include 
environmental health, planetary boundaries, the Anthropocene, the Great Acceleration, slow violence, 
toxic embodiment, environmental illness, solastalgia, ecological grief, climate anxiety, ecological 
distress, disaster trauma, environmental memory, multispecies health, transcorporeality, vulnerability, 
resilience, and planetary justice. Students will critically examine ecological distress while resisting its 
reduction to individual pathology and recognizing its social, ecological, and political dimensions. 
Literary and cultural texts are central. Novels, short fiction, poetry, film, visual culture, testimony, 
illness narratives, speculative fiction, ecocinema, and digital media will be read alongside scientific and 
policy discourses. Primary and theoretical materials may include Amitav Ghosh, Margaret Atwood, 
Richard Powers, Jeff VanderMeer, Kim Stanley Robinson, Paolo Bacigalupi, N. K. Jemisin, Indra 
Sinha, Donna Haraway, Stacy Alaimo, Rob Nixon, Dipesh Chakrabarty, Ashlee Cunsolo, Glenn 
Albrecht, Anna Tsing, Bruno Latour, Timothy Morton, Arturo Escobar, Achille Mbembe, and scholars 
of Planetary Health Humanities. The course also engages Indigenous, feminist, decolonial, and more-
than-human perspectives on health, care, and ecological reciprocity. 
At the advanced level, the course emphasizes conceptual synthesis, comparative analysis, close reading, 
interdisciplinary research design, and critical engagement with competing epistemologies of health. It 
culminates in the question of what it might mean to move from planetary illness toward planetary care, 
ecological justice, regenerative futures, and a more expansive conception of “cosmogenic health.” 
A) Objectives 
1. Critically examine the conceptual emergence of Planetary Health and its relationship to 
Environmental Humanities, Health Humanities, Medical Humanities, One Health, and EcoHealth. 
2. Develop a theoretically sophisticated understanding of “ecosickness” as a cultural, ecological, 
embodied and planetary condition. 
3. Interrogate the historical and philosophical separation of human health from environmental systems 
and critically assess human exceptionalism and biomedical reductionism. 
4. Examine how climate change, biodiversity loss, pollution, toxicity, extraction, altered disease 
ecologies and environmental disasters become embodied in individual and collective life. 
5. Engage major theoretical frameworks including slow violence, political ecology, environmental 
justice, transcorporeality, new materialism, posthumanism, multispecies studies and critical global 
health. 
6. Analyse ecological grief, solastalgia, climate anxiety and environmental distress without 
pathologizing legitimate responses to ecological loss. 
7. Study relationships among environmental change, social inequality, coloniality, caste, class, gender, 
indigeneity, displacement and structural vulnerability.

8. Explore planetary boundaries, Earth-system change, tipping points, planetary health governance and 
the ethics of sustaining human flourishing within ecological limits. 
9. Critically analyse literary, cinematic, visual, testimonial and digital representations of illness, 
contamination, disaster, pandemic, extinction, ecological grief and planetary futures. 
10. Develop interdisciplinary research questions and methodologies connecting cultural analysis with 
scientific, medical, ecological and policy knowledge. 
11. Examine Indigenous, feminist, relational, more-than-human and decolonial understandings of care, 
reciprocity and ecological wellbeing. 
12. Evaluate competing models of resilience, sustainability, adaptation, mitigation, regeneration, 
planetary justice and ecological care, and formulate theoretically grounded visions of healthier more-
than-human futures.

C) Prerequisites, if any 
There is no formal prerequisite. However, the course is intended for Ph.D. scholars in Humanities and 
Social Sciences, particularly, those working in English Literature, and advanced researchers working in 
the humanities, social sciences, environmental studies, health studies, public policy, science and 
technology studies, and allied interdisciplinary fields. Prior familiarity with critical theory, 
environmental humanities, health humanities, or environmental studies will be advantageous.

8) Other Remarks: This is a distinctive interdisciplinary course that brings Planetary Health and 
environmental illness into sustained dialogue with the Environmental Humanities. It foregrounds literary 
and cultural narratives as important modes of understanding environmental health, enabling meaningful 
interdisciplinary engagement with fields such as medicine, engineering, environmental science, and 
public policy. The course is designed to contribute to an emerging area of scholarship at the intersection 
of planetary health, environmental humanities, and health humanities.

6. Course Description: 
 
A) Objectives: 
This course analyses some plays of Shakespeare from a philosophical perspective. 
Shakespeare’s plays are rich not only dramatically or literarily but also philosophically. That 
doesn’t simply mean that they include, illustrate or embody philosophical ideas or that their 
poetic dimension is a dress of thought. Rather, the art of Shakespeare broadens our ken. His 
plays become philosophical clearings where we can reflect on enduring human concerns such 
as freedom and fate, individuality and society, longing and limits, enjoyment and suffering, 
sympathy and indifference, language and world, nature and artifice. The course attempts to see 
the aesthetic light that Shakespeare’s plays can shed on philosophising. The course principally 
considers four plays: Hamlet, Othello, Merchant of Venice, and Romeo and Juliet. The course 
draws on engagements of philosophers, literary critics, and writers with Shakespeare’s plays.

B) Contents (preferably in the form of 5 to 10 broad titles): 
 
1. Prologue: D. H. Lawrence, “When I Read Shakespeare”. Jeffrey Archer, “A Gentleman and 
a Scholar”. Anthony Burgess, “The Muse”. (2 lectures) 
2. Shakespeare’s art: drama, poetry, storytelling, philosophy (2 lectures) 
3. Aristotle’s Poetics and the poetics of Shakespeare’s plays (8 lectures) 
4. Collingwood’s and Tolstoy’s critiques of Shakespeare (2 lectures) 
5. Shakespearean tragedy and comedy (4 lectures) 
6. Shakespeare: language and world (4 lectures) 
7. Shakespeare: self, subjectivity, identity (5 lectures) 
8. Shakespeare and Montaigne: essay, thinking, ‘I’ (2 lectures) 
9. Shakespeare: believing, doubting, knowing (4 lectures) 
10. Shakespeare: desire, love, sex, society (5 lectures) 
11. Shakespeare: aesthetic autonomy and limits (2 lectures) 

C) Pre-requisites, if any (examples: a- PSO201A, or b- PSO201A or equivalent): None 
 
D) Short summary for including in the Courses of Study Booklet: 
This course analyses some plays of Shakespeare from philosophical points of view. It 
principally considers Hamlet, Othello, Merchant of Venice, and Romeo and Juliet. 
Shakespeare’s plays offer a philosophical space where we can reflect on enduring human 
concerns such as freedom and fate, individuality and society, longing and limits, enjoyment 
and suffering, sympathy and indifference, language and world, nature and artifice. The course 
examines the poetics of his plays; the nature of tragedy and comedy; the light they shed on self, 
subjectivity, knowledge, the relationship of language and world.

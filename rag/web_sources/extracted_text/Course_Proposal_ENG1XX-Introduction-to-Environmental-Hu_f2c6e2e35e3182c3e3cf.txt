6. Course Description 
This course introduces Environmental Humanities (EH) as an interdisciplinary field that brings the 
humanities into sustained conversation with environmental sciences and other modes of ecological 
inquiry. It examines how literature, film, visual culture, digital media, philosophy, history, anthropology, 
ethics and cultural practices shape understandings of nature, environment, climate and the more-than-
human world. The course asks how environmental crises are narrated, experienced, remembered and 
contested, and how humanities perspectives can contribute to environmental thought, justice and possible 
futures. 
 
The course begins by interrogating the nature–culture divide and the emergence of environmental 
humanities, before moving through ecocriticism, environmental imagination, place and landscape, the 
Anthropocene and its critical alternatives, environmental justice, postcolonial and decolonial ecologies, 
ecofeminism, posthumanism, new materialism and multispecies studies. It further examines 
environmental governance, ecological citizenship, Indigenous and traditional ecological knowledge 
systems, environmental communication, and public humanities approaches to ecological challenges. It 
also introduces focused approaches such as slow violence, toxicity and waste studies, Blue Humanities, 
environmental memory, ecocinema, ecomedia, ecopoetics and speculative/climate fiction. Attention is 
also given to contemporary concerns including extinction studies, energy humanities, disaster humanities, 
urban ecologies, and digital environmental cultures. 
Attention will be given to Indian and South Asian environmental contexts, including environmental 
movements, extractivism, pollution, water and agrarian crises, displacement, ecological inequality and the 
cultural politics of environmental change. Students will engage with literary texts, films, visual archives,

The course will ultimately consider questions of environmental ethics, stewardship, sustainability,

other cultural texts will serve as primary sites for developing critical environmental thinking. 
A) Objectives 
1. Introduce students to the emergence, scope, vocabulary and interdisciplinary character of 
Environmental Humanities. 
2. Examine how cultural narratives and aesthetic forms shape perceptions of nature, environment, 
climate change and ecological crisis. 
3. Develop familiarity with major approaches including ecocriticism, environmental ethics, the 
Anthropocene, environmental justice, ecofeminism, posthumanism, new materialism and multispecies 
studies. 
4. Interrogate the nature–culture divide and rethink human exceptionalism through more-than-human 
and relational approaches. 
5. Explore the spatial and temporal scales of environmental change, including place, landscape, 
bioregionalism, planetary thinking, slow violence and environmental memory. 
6. Examine the cultural, political and ethical dimensions of pollution, toxicity, waste, extraction, 
resource depletion and environmental health. 
7. Introduce Blue Humanities, ecomedia, ecocinema, ecopoetics and other emerging modes of 
environmental cultural analysis. 
8. Situate environmental humanities within Indian and South Asian contexts, with attention to 
environmental justice, colonialism, caste, class, gender, indigeneity and uneven vulnerability. 
9. Enable students to critically evaluate competing ideas of sustainability, conservation, stewardship, 
development and ecological futures. 
10. Develop the capacity to connect humanities-based environmental inquiry with ethical reflection, 
public engagement and imaginative forms of ecological worldmaking.

D) Short Summary for Courses of Study Booklet 
An interdisciplinary introduction to Environmental Humanities, this course examines how literature, film, 
visual culture, history, philosophy, ethics, media, and other cultural forms interpret, represent, and 
respond to environmental change and ecological crisis. It considers how culture shapes public 
understandings of nature, climate, development, technology, sustainability, and the relationship between 
human and more-than-human worlds. 
The course introduces major concepts and debates in the field, including ecocriticism, environmental 
imagination, environmental justice, Anthropocene studies, posthumanism, new materialism, multispecies 
studies, toxicity and waste, Blue Humanities, environmental memory, ecocinema, environmental media, 
Indigenous ecological knowledge, climate fiction, and ecological futures. Students explore how stories, 
images, archives, landscapes, and media practices reveal unequal environmental burdens, contested ideas 
of progress, and the ethical challenges posed by climate change, biodiversity loss, pollution, extraction, 
and resource depletion. 
Through global, Indian, and South Asian case studies, the course pays particular attention to the cultural 
and political dimensions of environmental inequality, displacement, disaster, water, forests, coasts, urban 
ecologies, and rural livelihoods. It examines how colonial histories, capitalism, caste, gender, class, race, 
indigeneity, and geopolitics influence both environmental harm and possibilities for ecological care. By 
bringing together critical theory with creative and cultural texts, students develop the tools to analyse 
sustainability, justice, responsibility, resilience, and collective futures in an era of rapid planetary 
transformation.

8. Other Remarks 
The course is designed as an interdisciplinary, introductory-level Environmental Humanities offering for 
undergraduate students across disciplines. It combines conceptual/theoretical discussion with close 
reading and interpretation of literary, cinematic, visual and other cultural texts. The design draws on 
established Environmental Humanities curricula that emphasize eco-storytelling, ecocinema and 
ecomedia, nature–culture relations, environmental justice, slow violence, multispecies coexistence, Blue 
Humanities, Indigenous/Asian ecological traditions, environmental ethics and possible futures. It also 
incorporates the literature-oriented strengths of existing environmental humanities courses, particularly 
environmental representation, environmental justice, global environmental literature and the intersections 
of ecological issues with race/class/gender/geographical location 
Pedagogically, the course may include short critical responses, close-reading exercises, visual/media 
analyses, group presentations and a final research or creative project. Such activities are consistent with 
the interdisciplinary and practice-oriented models represented in the supplied syllabi.

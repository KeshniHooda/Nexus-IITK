8.  Course Description:

Indian Logic (in Sanskrit anviksiki, nyaya, or tarka) is the tradition of systematic reasoning of the
Indian subcontinent, evolved over nearly two millennia from roughly the sixth century BCE onward. It
is distinctive in that it examines not only the forms of acceptable arguments, but also the sources of
valid cognition (pramanas), namely perception, inference, comparison and verbal testimony. Hence
logic, epistemology and language are viewed as one continuous study. Validity is analyzed in
connection with justified belief and reliable sources (including testimony, a category that Western
analytic philosophy has only recently considered respectable). The tradition matured in public
deliberative assemblies with formalized rules of debate, and it produced a rich technical apparatus:
the five-membered syllogism of the Nyaya school, the three-condition doctrine of the Buddhist
logicians, the sevenfold predication of Jainism (saptabhangi), sophisticated theories of truth and
error, a precise taxonomy of fallacies (hetvabhasa), and even explicitly non-classical, many-valued
modes of reasoning such as the Buddhist four-cornered analysis (catuskoti). This course aims to
provide students with the skills to understand, systemize, evaluate, and compare the primary Indian
logical frameworks, while recognizing their continued importance in contemporary intellectual
debate.

9. Course Objectives:

1. Introduce Indian logic: explain its nature and scope, and show how it is a distinct discipline that can
be compared with Western logic.
2. Define and use the Pramana framework. Reconstruct and test the Nyaya five‑membered syllogism
and the Buddhist trairupya conditions.
3. Identify fallacies with the hetvabhasa classification. Show how the catuskoti and the saptabhangi
work as principled non‑classical valuation schemes.
4. Compare Indian theories of truth, validity, and causation with classical and non‑classical Western
theories.
5. Relate these ideas to current research in non-classical logic, artificial intelligence, knowledge
representation, linguistics, and the epistemology of testimony.

C) Short summary for including in the Courses of Study Booklet:

Indian logic (known in Sanskrit as anviksiki, nyāya, or tarka) is a tradition of systematic reasoning that
emerged in the Indian subcontinent and evolved over roughly two millennia, beginning around the
sixth century BCE. This course introduces Indian traditions of logic and reasoning. The course surveys
key schools—Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya and Yoga, Pūrva Mīmāṃsā, Advaita Vedānta, Cārvāka,
Jainism, and Buddhist logic—emphasizing theories of knowledge (pramāṇa), inference (anumāna),
perception (pratyakṣa), testimony (śabda), and negation (abhāva). No prior background in philosophy,
Sanskrit, or logic is required. How do we come to know what we know, and what makes an argument
sound? It addresses both questions by exploring India’s rigorous traditions of inquiry, ranging from
the Cārvāka skeptics and the realist logicians of Nyāya to the Buddhist epistemologists and the Jain
philosophers who embrace many‑valued truth, and by engaging them in a careful dialogue with

[PAGE 4]
modern symbolic logic. The course culminates in a balanced assessment of five questions: Where do
Indian traditions resemble classical logic? Which are better compared with epistemic, modal, many-
valued, or defeasible frameworks? Why is Jaina syādvāda not simply many-valued logic? Is Indian
logic "epistemic logic"? And how do pramā-based accounts of knowledge compare with Plato's and
with the modern justified-true-belief model?
